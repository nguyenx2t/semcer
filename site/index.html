    Abstract

    Character Error Rate (CER), the standard metric for OCR and vision-language models (VLMs), charges a typo as much as a wrong name. We introduce semCER, which keeps CER's alignment and denominator and charges each grapheme edit a severity from an LLM judge, with no word segmentation. Normalized CER keeps the score of a whole transcription, where it tracks readers as well as semCER or better, and semCER breaks that score down into named edits. The charges track readers' ratings edit by edit in English, Vietnamese, and Chinese and put the edits readers rate most severe near the top of a sorted list. On pairs drawn where CER and semCER disagreed, readers side with semCER more often than with normalized CER in English and Chinese, and equally often in Vietnamese. Across 16 languages, the judge charges a wrong diacritic or tone mark almost nothing in Spanish but nearly a wrong letter's severity in Telugu. Where we can count real words, a general VLM's wrong letter forms one far above chance while a classical pipeline's stays near it. We release the 61 million judged edits, the aggregated reader ratings, and the distilled scorer.

    How it works

    Same alignment, new price tag

    semCER finds errors exactly the way CER does. It only changes what each one costs: an LLM judge reads the whole line and rates each error from 0 to 1, and a 0.8B model distilled from it does the same on your laptop. Flip between the two price lists.

      Where the two scores disagree, readers mostly side with semCER

      We gave readers two transcriptions of the same line that CER and semCER ranked in opposite order, and let them pick the better one.

      In English and Chinese, semCER matched their pick more often than CER did, even after CER ignores case, spacing and punctuation. In Vietnamese, the two tied.

      An accent is cheap in Spanish and costly in Telugu

      In Spanish, a dropped accent like más → mas costs almost nothing. In Telugu or Kannada, a wrong mark costs almost as much as a wrong letter.

      CER charges 1 either way. semCER follows what the mark does in each language.

      Why the spread? It follows what the marks do more than the type of script. A Spanish or French accent mostly decorates a letter. Vietnamese tone marks and the vowel signs of Hindi, Thai and Telugu change the word, so Vietnamese, an alphabet, sits next to Hindi.

      Vision-language models make wrong words, not just typos

      A classic OCR engine’s wrong letter usually makes a non-word that a spell checker would flag. A vision-language model’s wrong letter makes a real word far more often, one that reads fine and slips through. semCER charges its letter errors more.
